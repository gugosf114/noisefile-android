So You’ve Got a Barky Dog…

  • Report a barking dog
  • Respond to a complaint about your barking dog

Management:

Some dogs are reactive to visual stimuli and will bark at passersby seen through a window, a dog across the street, the mailman, etc.

  • Close your curtains when you are not available to actively manage your dog.
  • Toss treats to refocus your dog when they begin to react and bark.

Some dogs are reactive to auditory stimuli and will bark in response to noises they hear.

  • Close your windows when you are not available to actively manage your dog.
  • Try using a white noise machine, music, or television to mask some of the triggering noises your dog may be hearing.

Some dogs bark periodically when they are left home alone. This could be from stress or boredom.

Make sure you’ve met your dog’s needs physically and emotionally before you leave them alone.

  • Aerobic activity – a long walk, a romp in the yard, a good round of fetch, a trip to the park, whatever your dog prefers for physical activity.
  • Mental stimulation—a “sniffari” style walk where you let them sniff whatever they’d like can be extremely fulfilling for your dog and can help them decompress from stressful situations. A quick training session can be very stimulating for a dog as well.

Provide lots of enrichment for your dog when you are leaving the home.

    • Food puzzles
    • Kongs, Toppls, slow feeders, snuffle mats
    • Bully sticks, bones, rawhides, chews.
    • Toys
    • Cardboard boxes or DIY enrichment

 

Pro Tips:

    • Remember that all dogs have their own preferences for enrichment – try all kinds of things to learn what your dog enjoys best
    • Always make sure the enrichment you choose is able to be enjoyed safely when your dog is unsupervised.

 

Some dogs bark due to genuine separation anxiety.

  • This is different from boredom or barking for attention.
  • Dogs with separation anxiety often bark, whine, and howl incessantly when left home alone and may be destructive or self-injurious around the house.
  • Dogs with separation anxiety have a difficult time settling while alone. Some dogs will bark, howl, whine, and then settle. Dogs with separation anxiety rarely settle and are typically in a state of anxiety and panic when left alone.
  • If you’re not sure how your dog is doing home alone, consider buying a puppy camera. There are many models on the market today that allow a guardian an inside peek into what their dog is doing when home alone.

Separation anxiety can be treated!

  • Speak with your veterinarian about possible medications to assist your dog with alone time.
  • Hire a Certified Separation Anxiety Trainer also known as a CSAT or visit with a veterinary behaviorist.

Some dogs bark due to old age or medical reasons.

  • Dogs suffer from cognitive decline much like people do; canine cognitive dysfunction is often referred to as “doggy dementia” and can result in transient barking (barking that occurs seemingly without a discernible trigger)
  • Dogs may also bark due to discomfort – pain from arthritis, blindness or deafness, disorientation, elevated blood pressure, etc.
  • If you think your dog is barking due to discomfort or decreased cognitive function, consult with your vet.

Quick Training Tips:

Acclimate Your Dog to Sights and Sounds

If your dog barks in response to certain stimuli, you can desensitize and counter-condition them to the particular sights and sounds that trigger their barking. Some dogs have an easier time getting used to new sounds, while others need more time to learn. Work on creating a positive association with sights and sounds at which your dog usually barks – the mailman, a delivery person, the doorbell, the sounds of your upstairs neighbors.

Let’s use the example of the mailman dropping off mail at your home. Dogs typically alert bark because they are feeling fearful about a particular stimulus – if you teach your dog that there is nothing to fear when the mailman arrives, they will not feel the need to bark!

  • When you’re not training, make sure you’ve drawn the curtains or blocked your dog’s ability to see someone passing by. If your dog likes to sit at a bench at the window, move the bench!
  • When you’re actively training, grab a few high-value training treats. You should be using something extra yummy and appealing, not your everyday treats.
  • The moment your dog notices the mailman, but before they begin barking, say “yes” (or click if you use a clicker) and toss them a treat in the opposite direction (away from the window).
    • This will help to break up the pattern of repetitive barking and allow the dog to refocus elsewhere.
  • If they look right back at the mailman, say “yes” or click again before they bark, and toss another treat away from the stimulus.

With time and practice, you will have taught your dog an alternative behavior to alert barking – looking at you instead! You have also taught your dog that good things happen when the mailman arrives.

What Not to Do:

When your dog begins to bark, do not yell at them.

  • For dogs that are barking for attention, yelling at them can actually reinforce the barking behavior. Though the attention being provided is negative, you are still giving your dog attention, which is reinforcing.
  • For dogs that are barking due to fear or reactivity, you are compounding your dog’s stress response to a particular stimulus—effectively teaching them that yes, that mailman is indeed cause for concern.

Use an anti-bark collar, spray a barking dog with water, or other aversive methods.

  • Using aversive stimuli such as pain or discomfort (electric shock or citronella spray) can exacerbate behavior issues.
  • If a dog is barking at a particular stimulus because it is fearful, the addition of pain or discomfort teaches the dog that the stimulus is something to fear; if your dog barks because they are afraid of the mailman, and then gets an electric shock when they see the mailman, they will associate the mailman with pain. This will increase their fear and reactivity.
  • You are not addressing the root of the behavior (how the dog feels about a stimulus), you are merely suppressing the behavior.
  • This means the dog will resume barking once bark collar/aversive stimuli is removed.
